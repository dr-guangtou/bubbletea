Necessary background: compact galaxies hidden among stars

Course guide · Objects to investigate · Instructor source checks

Why look for ultra-compact dwarf galaxies?

Galaxies grow through interactions and mergers. Some of their history survives in small stellar systems orbiting them. A compact object that looks unremarkable in an image can therefore carry evidence about how a much larger galaxy assembled. UCDs are interesting partly because their origin is not unique: some may be very massive star clusters, while others may be galaxy centers left behind after their outer stars were stripped away. Identifying the objects is the first step toward testing those histories, not the final answer.

M60-UCD1 makes this question tangible. Detailed follow-up found a massive black hole in this small stellar system, supporting an origin as the surviving center of a larger galaxy. This result required spectroscopy and dynamical modeling; students cannot infer a black hole from a Legacy Survey cutout or a Gaia fit statistic. See Seth et al. (2014).

There is also a lesson about how discoveries happen. Bright compact systems can be overlooked when a survey labels them as stars or when a search assumes that all interesting galaxies must look visibly extended. M59-UCD3 was found by combining archival images and spectroscopy rather than accepting a single catalog label. Its discovery study is a useful example of asking a new question of existing data.

In this short project we will not establish a new object's distance, formation history, or UCD classification. We will learn to identify useful evidence, find obvious alternative explanations, and recommend what should be checked next.

1. What kind of object are we looking for?

A foreground star belongs to the Milky Way. A globular cluster is a dense group of stars. A UCD is a compact stellar system that can be larger or more luminous than ordinary globular clusters, although the categories overlap. A host galaxy is the larger galaxy with which a compact system may be physically associated. Being near that galaxy on the sky does not prove association.

Some UCDs may be stripped galaxy nuclei: compact centers left behind after a larger galaxy loses its outer stars. Do not teach that every UCD has this origin or that a single size or brightness boundary settles the classification.

Naming trap: Gaia also uses “UCD” for ultracool dwarfs, very cool stellar objects. Its gold_sample_ucd table is not a catalog of ultra-compact dwarf galaxies. See the Gaia table definition.

2. A point-like image does not imply a single star

A telescope blurs a true point source into a small patch. That pattern is called the point-spread function, or PSF. An object is resolved only when its own spatial extent can be distinguished from this blur. Atmospheric seeing contributes to the blur in ground-based images. Image pixel size and resolving power are not the same thing.

At larger distance the same physical object subtends a smaller angle. Thus a nearby UCD can look extended while a similar, more distant UCD looks like a star. Compare against neighboring stars in the same image, not against images with different seeing or display stretches. “Unresolved” is a valid observation, not a rejection of a UCD.

Physical sizes and distances here use parsecs (pc), an astronomical distance unit; one megaparsec (Mpc) is one million parsecs.

Worked example: the discovery paper gives M60-UCD1 a half-light radius of 24.2 pc, the radius enclosing half its measured light, at an adopted distance of 16.5 Mpc. The small-angle relation gives

angular radius (arcseconds) = 206265 × radius (pc) / distance (pc)
                           = 206265 × 24.2 / 16,500,000
                           = 0.303 arcseconds, rounded

This is a half-light radius, not a diameter or the observed image width. A ground-based image can therefore look nearly point-like without contradicting the resolved measurements from better imaging. These literature inputs are from Strader et al. (2013).

Why Gaia favors star-like objects - but does not see only stars

Gaia scanned the sky rather than taking ordinary large photographs for each target. Its onboard software searched for compact concentrations of light and allocated small detector regions, called windows, for measurements and transmission to Earth. Rejecting very sharp detector events and broad diffuse signals helped make autonomous detection practical. A smooth, faint galaxy disk is therefore much less favored than a compact bright center or a UCD.

The software detects a light pattern, not the object's physical identity. A quasar, compact galaxy, or galaxy nucleus can enter the catalog if it has a suitable bright compact component. An extended object need not be perfectly point-like. Gaia's ground processing can subsequently show departures from a stellar image or motion model. That is the opportunity this project explores, but also the reason blends and image problems must be considered. See de Bruijne et al. (2015) and the DR3 extended-object summary.

What is Gaia's spatial resolution?

Here spatial resolution means an angular separation on the sky, not a physical length in parsecs. There is no single number that describes detection, image sampling, resolving two sources, and final catalog completeness.

Quantity Useful numerical context What it means
Detector pixel sampling 0.0589 arcsec along the scan; 0.1768 arcsec across it Angular width of one detector pixel, not the resolving limit
Onboard equal-brightness pair resolution About 0.23 arcsec along the scan and 0.70 arcsec across it in the detection study Two equally bright point sources can be separated more easily in one direction; unequal brightness can require more separation
Published catalog pair completeness EDR3 validation finds losses below about 1.5 arcsec, becoming severe below about 0.7 arcsec The catalog does not contain every close pair that the optics could in principle distinguish

“Along the scan” means the direction an object's image moves across Gaia's detector; “across” is perpendicular to it. Different visits have different scan directions. The pixel values are from the mission instrument description, the detection numbers from de Bruijne et al., and the catalog limits from Fabricius et al. (2021), Section 2.3. The last result concerns EDR3; DR3 uses the EDR3 astrometric source solution. None of these pair-separation numbers is a cutoff on UCD half-light radius.

Positional precision is different: repeated measurements can locate the center of a blurred source much more precisely than the width of the blur. Very precise positions do not mean Gaia resolves individual stars inside a distant UCD. Gaia normally records their combined light as one source, if it detects that system.

3. Apparent brightness is not luminosity

Magnitude tells us how bright something appears in a particular filter. To infer intrinsic brightness we need distance and, for precise work, extinction and appropriate photometry. For negligible extinction,

absolute magnitude = apparent magnitude − 5 log10(distance in pc / 10)

Use this as a concept, not a Gaia-only UCD definition. Gaia G, Legacy Survey g, and literature V are different filters. Gaia's measurement of a slightly extended object may also miss light compared with a total-light measurement. Do not insert Gaia G into a published V-band classification threshold as though they were the same measurement. An assumed host distance gives a conditional luminosity, not independent proof that the source belongs to that host.

4. Parallax and proper motion are measurements with uncertainty

Astrometry means measuring positions and how they change. Parallax is the annual apparent positional shift associated with the Earth's orbit. Proper motion is a change in sky position over time. Gaia reports parallax in milliarcseconds (mas) and proper motion in mas per year; 1 arcsecond is 1000 mas. Nearby stars can have measurable values; a distant compact galaxy is expected to have values far below Gaia's practical precision.

The field pmra is the east-west component, including the cosine of declination so it represents an angular motion on the sky, not simply the change in RA angle. The field pmdec is the north-south component. Both are in mas per year.

For parallax, a useful warning measure is abs(parallax) / parallax_error. A value near one is not convincing evidence for a nonzero parallax. Negative measured parallaxes can arise from noise and calibration; they are not negative physical distances. Do not invert an uncertain or negative parallax to estimate the distance to a UCD.

For the live M60-UCD1 Gaia record, parallax is −0.079 ± 0.395 mas: its absolute significance is 0.20. Its two proper-motion components are +0.420 ± 0.475 and −0.547 ± 0.432 mas/year. These are consistent with no detected motion, not proof of a galaxy. A distant star, QSO, compact background galaxy, or poor measurement can also have no convincing detected motion.

A missing parallax or motion means Gaia did not supply that measurement. It does not mean the measurement was exactly zero. The optional combined-motion calculation, which includes the correlation between the two components, is in the instructor record.

5. Gaia can notice a poor stellar fit

Gaia fits a model to its observations. A source's actual shape, a neighboring source, orbital motion in a multiple-star system, or other measurement problems can make a stellar model fit poorly. This is why unusual Gaia measurements can help find non-stellar objects, but cannot identify UCDs by themselves.

Field Plain meaning What it does not establish
ruwe Renormalized unit weight error: a normalized measure of the astrometric fit quality A large value does not uniquely mean extension or a binary
astrometric_excess_noise Additional angular scatter used to account for residuals in the astrometric fit, in mas Not a direct size measurement
astrometric_excess_noise_sig Gaia's internal significance statistic for that extra scatter Not automatically a number of Gaussian standard deviations
phot_bp_rp_excess_factor Ratio of summed BP and RP flux to G flux Not a UCD probability or a universal pass/fail boundary

These field definitions come from the official Gaia DR3 source data model. Further project definitions and limitations are in Gaia features. Leave the image-window diagnostics, machine-learning details, and fit-solution codes to the instructor notes in this short course.

6. Why compare G magnitude with flux excess?

The raw flux-excess factor is

C = (I_BP + I_RP) / I_G

Here I means measured flux, not magnitude. BP and RP are Gaia's blue and red photometric measurements and G is its broad-band measurement. Their response and measurement regions differ. C is not expected to be exactly one for ordinary stars; its normal distribution depends on color and measurement quality.

A G-versus-C diagram helps compare a target with the distribution of ordinary sources. M60-UCD1 has C = 4.65, while the selected foreground comparison has C = 1.22. That contrast is useful, but extension, crowding, bright host light, and measurement problems can all affect C. Compare similar-color sources and inspect images. Do not derive a universal threshold from these two examples or confuse raw C with a color-corrected excess statistic.

7. Matching and provenance are part of the science

The nearest catalog row is not necessarily the intended object. Check the coordinates, angular separation, image center, nearby alternatives, and coordinate epoch, the date to which a position applies. A moving star can change position between surveys. Keep Gaia's long source identifier as text so spreadsheet rounding does not corrupt it.

Provenance means where a claim or measurement came from: the original paper, table, survey release, source identifier, and matching method. An object name alone can be ambiguous. Two papers calling an object “UCD1” need not refer to the same coordinates. Preserve the original source rather than silently correcting it.

8. Separate observation, interpretation, and confirmation

  • Observation: “The image is compact; no convincing motion is measured; the Gaia fit is poor; there is nearby host light.”
  • Interpretation: “It is worth checking as a compact extragalactic candidate, but a blend or background source is still possible.”
  • Confirmation: independent evidence, typically including spectroscopy for velocity/association and suitable imaging or other physical characterization. The precise classification can remain uncertain even with a measured velocity.

A quasar, also called a quasi-stellar object (QSO), is an active galactic nucleus that can also appear point-like and have no detectable motion. An H II region is gas ionized by young stars, often part of a galaxy rather than an independent UCD. A non-single-star catalog records Gaia evidence or solutions involving multiple stars. These are possible competing explanations, not automatic ground truth from a catalog label.

For the class, the required conclusion is a defensible evidence record and a follow-up recommendation. “Cannot tell from these data” can be the correct answer.

Task 02 / design a selection

From thirty objects
to a crowded catalog.

Can you write rules that recover UCDs without selecting too many stars and QSOs? Explore, evaluate, revise. The points begin without class labels.

Your goal: improve F1. This score combines the purity of your selected reference objects with the fraction of reference UCDs you recover. A strong result needs both high purity and high completeness. Keep trying, compare your attempts, then choose one final selection and explain it.

Purity, completeness, and the combined F1 score

Completeness is the fraction of all securely classified reference UCDs that your rule selects. Reference-subset purity is the fraction of selected securely classified objects that are UCDs, rather than stars or QSOs. F1 is their harmonic mean: it is closer to the smaller of the two than an ordinary average would be. The scale is 0 to 1, with higher values better.

F1=2PCP+C

Here P is purity and C is completeness, written as fractions. In words: multiply purity by completeness, double it, then divide by their sum. Selecting everything sacrifices purity; selecting only one genuine UCD sacrifices completeness. Neither automatically earns a high F1.

Unconfirmed candidates are shown and selected too, but their unknown classes enter none of these three scores. Their selected count is reported separately. Missing measurements remain in the catalog and reference denominators. Empty scored selections have undefined purity and F1 = 0; recovering no reference UCDs gives F1 = 0.

The sample is deliberately star-rich, drawn from literature objects and SDSS spectroscopy, not an unbiased sky census. Scores after repeated tuning describe this exercise, not independent performance in a new galaxy field. Higher F1 is the learning objective, not proof that any individual candidate is a UCD. Optional technical definition of F1.

Optional: why not use area under a curve here?

A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve follows a ranked score through many possible thresholds. Its area, ROC AUC, summarizes that ranking. Here you are making one selected/not-selected list with ranges, not assigning each object a continuous score. We therefore optimize F1 for that list. A high ROC AUC would not by itself promise a pure list in a star-rich search. ROC AUC and purity–completeness curves are optional further reading.
